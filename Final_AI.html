1. Introduction

1.1. Background and Motivation

With approximately 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2022 (WHO (2025)), breast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide. It is known to have a large impact on the global number of cancer deaths, and is projected to increase by around 40% by 2040, reaching nearly 3 million new cases per year (Arnold et al. (2022)). In Switzerland, the proportion of diagnoses among women under age 40 is increasing (RTS (2025)). These figures highlight the magnitude of breast cancer as a major global public health concern, reinforcing the importance of early detection, which plays a pivotal role in improving patient outcomes and supporting more refined risk assessment strategies.

Mammography is the cornerstone of population-wide screening programs, yet individual risk is highly heterogeneous. It varies according to a combination of demographic, clinical, and reproductive factors. Age, body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, family or personal history of breast cancer, hormone replacement therapy use, and breast density are among the most influential determinants shaping individual risk profiles (Obeagu & Obeagu (2024)). Other lifestyle-related factors, such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical inactivity, are also known to influence risk but are not captured in the dataset used for this study (Lukasiewicz et al. (2021)).

From a data science perspective, understanding how these factors interact is critical to refining early detection strategies and improving risk stratification models. The ability to quantify risk factors and understand their combined effects supports better resource allocation and predictive assessment, both in public health and in insurance domains where early detection and prevention can substantially reduce long-term costs.

1.2. Project Objectives

The overarching goal of this project is to characterize and quantify the demographic, clinical, and reproductive factors associated with two core indicators of breast cancer risk:

  • breast density (BI-RADS 3-4)
  • prior breast cancer history

This project is exploratory in nature, combining statistical analysis and visualization techniques to reveal meaningful patterns and associations within a large population dataset. Specifically, the project pursues the following objectives:

  1. Describe the population undergoing mammography between 2005 and 2017 by conducting an exploratory data analysis (EDA), focusing on the distribution of key risk factors.
  2. Investigate univariate and bivariate associations of demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics with the two outcomes of interest.
  3. Examine potential interactions, such as age × menopausal status or BMI × breast density.
  4. Analyze temporal trends in breast density and prior breast cancer to determine whether population-level patterns have shifted over time.
  5. Fit logistic regression models to quantify the adjusted effect of each factor on breast density and prior breast cancer, providing interpretable statistical insights.

The findings aim to give a better understanding of how each factor influences disease patterns, thereby contributing to more accurate assessment of risk profiles in female populations.

1.3. Research Questions

To operationalize these objectives, the study focuses on the following research questions:

1. What is the prevalence of high breast density and prior breast cancer history in the study population ?

Understanding the prevalence provides essential baseline information about the study population. It allows us to quantify how common high breast density and previous breast cancer diagnoses are, which is critical for identifying at-risk subgroups and contextualizing subsequent analyses.

2. How do demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics influence breast density levels ?

Breast density is a known risk factor for breast cancer, but its distribution can vary based on age, hormonal status, reproductive history, and other clinical factors. Investigating these relationships helps identify which characteristics are most predictive of higher breast density and can inform targeted screening or prevention strategies.

3. Which factors show the strongest association with a previous breast cancer diagnosis, and how do they interact ?

Identifying the factors most strongly linked to prior breast cancer can help prioritize risk assessment and guide personalized patient management.

These questions guide the entire analytical workflow and ensure a clear link between the dataset, the methods, and the final interpretation.

1.4. Related Work

Extensive research has established the role of demographic, reproductive, and clinical factors in shaping breast cancer risk. Classical epidemiological studies, such as those by Boyd et al. (2007) and McCormack & Santos Silva (2006), highlighted the strong association between breast density and breast cancer incidence, framing breast density as a key intermediate biomarker.

From a methodological standpoint, recent studies have leveraged data-driven and actuarial approaches to model cancer risk. Machine learning frameworks (e.g., Yala et al. (2022)) have demonstrated improved prediction of breast cancer outcomes using large-scale imaging and clinical datasets. These approaches align with actuarial principles of risk classification and model-based decision-making, emphasizing the integration of statistical learning into preventive health analytics.

The current study builds upon these findings by combining large-scale EDA with interpretable statistical models to provide a holistic, data-driven perspective on breast cancer risk patterns within the BCSC dataset.

2. Data

2.1. Data Source

This project relies on data from the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (BCSC), accessed on october 2025. The BCSC is a collaborative network of six active breast imaging registries and two historic registries focused on research to assess and improve the delivery and quality of breast cancer screening and related outcomes in the United States.

The BCSC proposes three datasets corresponding to different collection periods (2005 to 2009, 2009 to 2013, and 2013 to 2017) that we merged to obtained a unified dataset, which aggregates mammography records collected across multiple screening centers between 2005 and 2017, covering 6,788,438 mammograms.

The variables encompass:

  • Age group
  • Race and ethnicity
  • Family history of breast cancer
  • Age at first menstruation
  • Age at first birth
  • Breast density (BI-RADS)
  • Current hormone replacement therapy (HRT) use
  • Menopausal status
  • Body Mass Index (BMI)
  • Previous breast biopsy or aspiration
  • History of prior breast cancer
  • Year of screening

This breadth of variables allows for a comprehensive exploration of risk factors commonly highlighted in epidemiological literature. The dataset is fully anonymized and contains no personally identifiable information.

2.2. Dataset Overview

The 6.8 million mammography records were consolidated into aggregated subgroups, each representing a unique combination of demographic, clinical and reproductive factors. This aggregation proess results in a dataset containing approximately 1.7 million observations.

Key characteristics:

  • High-dimensional categorical structure: Most predictors are ordinal or categorical rather than continuous.
  • Consistent coding across years: Variable definitions remain stable from 2005 to 2017.
  • Large sample size: Enables robust estimation even in smaller subgroups.
  • Biologically meaningful variables: The variables have well-established links to breast cancer risk.

The initial dataset, an extract of which is presented in Table 1, contains 13 variables and 1,522,340 observations, amounting to 19,790,420 values.

2.4. Challenges

While the dataset is comprehensive and large-scale, several challenges limit the scope of the analysis :

  • The most significant limitaion lies in its aggregated structure, which prevents patient-level investigation and reduces the granularity of possible analyses
  • Several variables contain “unknown” labels or missing values, which may affect statistical robustness.
  • Some combinations of demographic and clinical factors are rare, resuting in uneven category representation. This may lead to:
    • Sparsity in specific subgroups
    • Wide confidence intervals in logistic regression
  • The race and ethnicity variable cannot be used for inferential analysis because of the strong overrepresentation of white non-hispanic women in the dataset, which could introduce substantial bias in population-level comparisons.

These factors must be considered when interpreting the results of the exploratory analysis and logistic regression modeling.

We will explore more concretely these challenges in the EDA section.

3. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

3.1. Purpose

The purpose of the Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is to gain a comprehensive understanding of the BCSC dataset before conducting any formal statistical modelling. This preliminary step allows us to:

  • Visualize the distribution of key demographic and clinical variables.
  • Examine potential structural biases or data quality issue that could affect subsequent analyses.
  • Uncover preliminary associations between predictors and outcomes.
  • Contextualize the magnitude and direction of expected effects.

Because the dataset contains over 1.7 million screening exams, EDA is essential to correctly characterize the profiles of women with high breast density or a personal history of breast cancer, to explore how major determinants such as age, BMI, menopausal status, family history, and reproductive factors relate to these outcomes, and to avoid misinterpretation in later stages.

These insights guide the selection and engineering of variables for modelling, support decisions regarding model specification and ensure that subsequent analyses are built on a solid empirical foundation.

3.2. Univariate Analysis

3.2.1. Distribution of Key Predictors

We begin by examinig the univariate distributions of the predictors. This examination provides the foundation for understanding risk dynamics in the screened population. Here are the relevant information we can conclude from it:

  • Age distribution: Following the expected age structure of a screening cohort, most women fall between 40 and 65 years, with a notable decline in participation after age 70. This pattern aligns with screening guidelines recommending regular mammography starting at age 40 or 45 and more selective screening in older age groups (Force (2024)).
  • Race and ethnicity distribution: There is a noticeable overrepresentation of non-Hispanic white women, which limits the scope of ethnic comparisons and requires cautious interpretation of this variable.
  • Menopausal status distribution: Post-menopausal women represent the majority of the sample, while surgical menopause is rare.
  • BMI distribution: BMI follows a right-skewed profile, with most women classified as normal or overweight.

4. Analysis

The analytical component of the project seeks to move beyond descriptive observations and to quantify the independent contribution of demographic, hormonal, and anthropometric factors to the two critical outcomes.

4.1. Selected Statistical Methods

To investigate the determinants of high breast density (BI-RADS 3–4) and prior breast cancer history, we employ multivariate logistic regression.

Multivariate logistic regression is a well suited method for binary outcomes that supports the inclusion of multiple categorical and ordinal predictors and yields interpretable odds ratios, which provide interpretable effect estimates and directly inform clinical or risk-stratification decisions.

Two separate models are estimated:

  • Model A: Probability of having high breast density
  • Model B: Probability of having a prior breast cancer diagnosis

4.2. Rationale for Method Choice

The choice of logistic regression is guided by insights from the exploratory data analysis:

  • The EDA reveals strong univariate gradients, such as:
    • The steady decrease of breast density with age and BMI.
    • The higher prevalence of prior breast cancer among post-menopausal and surgically menopausal women.
  • These patterns require multivariable adjustment to determine whether associations persist when controlling for correlated patient characteristics.

Moreover:

  • All predictors (age groups, BMI groups, menopausal status, reproductive factors, family history) are categorical or ordinal, which logistic regression handles naturally after appropriate one-hot encoding.
  • Correlation analysis indicated low multicollinearity, supporting the stability of coefficient estimation. However, as expected, a strong correlation was observed between age and menopausal status, reflecting their natural biological relationship. This implies that coefficients associated with menopause may partly reflect residual age effects. Therefore, menopause-related estimates are interpreted with caution and framed as adjusted associations rather than causal effects.
  • For Model B, the pronounced class imbalance motivates the use of imbalance-robust metrics and a comparison with SMOTE. Importantly, SMOTE is treated strictly as a modelling tool to improve classification sensitivity, and its implications for interpretability are explicitly considered in the discussion.

Given these conditions, logistic regression represents a robust, transparent, and theoretically grounded modelling strategy for both outcomes.

Model B examines the determinants of prior breast cancer history, defined as a binary indicator equal to 1 if a woman reports a previous breast cancer diagnosis and 0 otherwise. The same set of variables than model A was used with one additional predictor : age at menarche (age_menarche).

4.5. Key takeaways from the analysis

The modelling phase translates descriptive associations into statistically validated insights, enabling a rigorous understanding of Overall, the modelling phase confirms that several of the strongest visual patterns identified in the EDA persist after multivariable adjustment—most consistently for age and BMI, across both outcomes. Additionally, it provides a statistical foundation for constructing risk profiles and interpreting clinical determinants of breast density and breast cancer history, and identifies magnitude and direction of each predictor’s effect through odd ratios. Thus, the modelling phase enable a rigorous understanding of the factors shaping breast cancer risk and mammographic patterns in the population.

Model A (High breast density): Estimated odds ratios indicate a clear age gradient, with substantially lower odds of BI-RADS 3–4 density at older ages, and an inverse association with BMI, consistent with the descriptive density patterns observed earlier. Model A demonstrates moderate discrimination as reflected by ROC-AUC. Importantly, the multivariate framework confirms that the strong age and BMI gradients observed in the EDA are not driven by confounding with menopausal or reproductive factors, but persist as independent associations after joint adjustment.

Model B (Prior breast cancer history): Even after SMOTE, predictive performance remains modest (AUC ≈ 0.68), which is expected given that prior breast cancer is a rare outcome (~6%) and is influenced by risk drivers not captured in our feature set (e.g., genetics, detailed clinical history, treatment trajectories, and environmental exposures). In this setting, SMOTE can improve sensitivity to minority cases but may also increase false positives, highlighting the difficulty of rare-event prediction using only demographic and reproductive variables.

Taken together, these results support logistic regression as a strong choice for transparent inference and effect-size reporting in our project context, while also clarifying the limits of purely predictor-based risk classification for complex, multifactorial outcomes.

5. Conclusions

After defining the study objectives and preparing the dataset through systematic cleaning, harmonization, and encoding, the exploratory analysis revealed clear demographic, hormonal, and reproductive patterns shaping both breast density and prior breast cancer history. These patterns aligned closely with established epidemiological knowledge, particularly regarding age, BMI, menopausal status, and reproductive history.

First research question : What is the prevalence of high breast density and prior breast cancer history in the study population ?

The analysis estimates that high breast density affects approximately half of the screened population,whereas prior breast cancer history is observed in a much smaller share (~6%). This large prevalence gap is substantively important: high breast density represents a common characteristic relevant for screening stratification, while prior breast cancer history is a relatively rare outcome. From a modelling standpoint, the low base rate of prior breast cancer directly motivated the use of imbalance-aware performance metrics and the evaluation of SMOTE to improve minority-class detection in Model B.

Second research question : How do demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics influence breast density levels ?

The exploratory analysis and Model A provide a clear answer to this question: breast density is primarily driven by age and BMI, with a pronounced decline in density observed among older women and those with higher BMI. Menopausal status further stratifies this pattern, with post-menopausal women exhibiting substantially lower breast density than pre-menopausal women, even after accounting for age. Reproductive characteristics, such as age at first birth, show more modest but consistent associations, indicating secondary influences rather than primary drivers. Importantly, these effects remain statistically significant in the multivariable model, demonstrating that the observed relationships are not explained by confounding but reflect robust, biologically grounded associations.

Third research question: Which factors show the strongest association with a previous breast cancer diagnosis, and how do they interact ?

Model B indicates that age and menopausal status are the strongest predictors of a previous breast cancer diagnosis, reflecting the cumulative nature of cancer risk over the life course as well as screening-related selection effects. In contrast, first-degree family history—typically a major risk factor—exhibits a weaker and even negative adjusted association in the multivariable model. This result should not be interpreted as an epidemiological reversal. Rather, it reflects methodological and structural constraints of the dataset.

Specifically, the strong correlation between age and personal cancer history, combined with the substantial reduction of the analytic sample (from over 6 million to approximately 345,000 observations) and the application of SMOTE to address outcome imbalance, likely redistributed explanatory variance across predictors. As a result, once the highly dominant effects of age and menopausal status were controlled for, the residual signal attributed to family history became unstable and sensitive to data structure. Consequently, while Model B robustly identifies the primary determinants of prior breast cancer history, the estimated effects of secondary predictors must be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect artefacts of aggregation, selection, and rebalancing rather than underlying etiological mechanisms.

Together, the EDA and multivariable modelling phases offer a robust analytic framework for understanding the prevalence and determinants of both breast density and prior breast cancer history. Although the models do not capture all dimensions of breast cancer risk, particularly socioeconomic and behavioral factors absent from the dataset, they provide a solid statistical foundation for interpreting the observed relationships and for advancing toward final analytical and clinical insights.

Taken together, these results show that while the modelling framework is well suited to identifying the dominant, population-level drivers of breast cancer history, it also highlights how data structure and outcome rarity constrain the interpretability of secondary risk factors in observational settings.

5.1. Limitations

Despite the strengths of the analytical approach, several important limitations must be acknowledged:

  1. Selection bias from dataset reduction (6M -> 345k)

The original dataset contained over six million observations, but preprocessing steps reduced the analytic sample to approximately 345,000. This substantial reduction may have introduced selection bias, disproportionately altering the distribution of key variables such as family history or reproductive factors. The unexpected behavior of certain coefficients most notably the weak or negative effect of first-degree family history may be partly explained by this distortion.

  1. Structural imbalances in the dataset

The dataset exhibited meaningful structural characteristics that may limit generalizability, including:

  • Racial imbalance
  • Unequal representation across age groups
  • Clinical selection effects tied to screening patterns

These patterns reflect the fact that the dataset originates from breast imaging clinics rather than a population-based sample.

  1. Impact of SMOTE on epidemiological validity

To address the severe class imbalance in Model B (prior breast cancer history), SMOTE oversampling was applied. While appropriate from a machine learning standpoint, SMOTE can generate synthetic observations that dilute or obscure subtle clinical signals. This likely contributed to the instability of coefficients for lower-prevalence predictors.

  1. Socioeconomic and behavioral confounding

Breast cancer risk is strongly influenced by non-biological factors that were not present in the dataset, such as:

  • Access to screening
  • Lifestyle variables (alcohol use, physical activity)
  • Environmental exposures

Because breast imaging in the U.S. is costly and strongly insurance-dependent, the dataset likely overrepresents higher-income women who undergo routine screening. This limits the applicability of the findings to the general U.S. population.

  1. Model scope and prediction limitations

Model B displayed modest discriminatory performance (AUC ~0.68), suggesting that the available predictors explain only part of the variance in prior breast cancer history. This reinforces the influence of unmeasured confounders and the inherent limitations of the dataset.

Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the value of combining large-scale exploratory analysis with interpretable statistical models to uncover robust population-level patterns in breast cancer risk, and provides a strong analytical foundation for future work integrating richer clinical, genetic, and behavioral data.
