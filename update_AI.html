1. Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation

Breast cancer is the most common cancer worldwide, with approximately 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2022, and remains the leading cause of cancer-related death among women (WHO (2025)). In Switzerland, the proportion of diagnoses among women under 40 years old is increasing (RTS (2025)). According to recent projections, its global incidence could increase by more than 40% by 2040, reaching nearly 3 million new cases per year (Arnold et al. (2022)).

These figures highlight the magnitude of breast cancer as a major global public health concern. Early detection, particularly through mammography, plays a pivotal role in improving patient outcomes. However, breast cancer risk is not homogeneous: it varies according to a combination of demographic, clinical, and reproductive factors. Age, body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, family or personal history of breast cancer, hormone replacement therapy use, and breast density are among the most influential determinants shaping individual risk profiles (Obeagu & Obeagu (2024)). Other lifestyle-related factors, such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical inactivity, are also known to infuence risk but are not captured in the dataset used for this study (Lukasiewicz et al. (2021)). Understanding how these factors interact is key to refining early detection strategies and improving risk stratification models.

From a data science and actuarial perspective, exploring these associations provides valuable insights into risk modeling, population segmentation, and preventive health analytics. The ability to quantify risk factors and understand their combined effects supports better resource allocation and predictive assessment, both in public health and in insurance domains where early detection and prevention can substantially reduce long-term costs.

1.2 Project Objectives

The main goal of this project is to explore the relationships between demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics and two key indicators of breast cancer risk: breast density and prior breast cancer history.

This project is exploratory in nature, combining statistical analysis and visualization techniques to reveal meaningful patterns and associations within a large population dataset. Specifically, the study aims to :

  1. Conduct an exploratory data analysis (EDA) to characterize the distribution of key demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics among women undergoing mammography between 2005 and 2017.
  2. Investigate associations between breast density, a known intermediate marker of breast cancer risk, and prior breast cancer history to better understand their co-occurence and potential shared determinants.
  3. Explore interaction effects between key factors. For instance, between age and menopausal status, body mass index and breast density, or breast density and age.
  4. Analyze temporal trends to identify how the characteristics of women with high breast density or prior cancer have evolved over time.
  5. Use logistic regression modeling to quantify the relative contribution of each factor to both breast density and prior breast cancer, providing interpretable statistical insights.

The findings aim to give a better understanding of how each factor influences disease patterns, thereby contributing to more accurate assessment of risk profiles in female populations.

1.3 Research Questions

To operationalize these objectives, the following research questions guide this study. Each question has been formulated to ensure a clear link between the dataset, the study population, and the overarching goal of understanding risk factors related to breast density and breast cancer history.

1. What is the prevalence of high breast density and prior breast cancer history in the study population ?

Understanding the prevalence provides essential baseline information about the study population. It allows us to quantify how common high breast density and previous breast cancer diagnoses are, which is critical for identifying at-risk subgroups and contextualizing subsequent analyses.

2. How do demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics influence breast density levels ?

Breast density is a known risk factor for breast cancer, but its distribution can vary based on age, hormonal status, reproductive history, and other clinical factors. Investigating these relationships helps identify which characteristics are most predictive of higher breast density and can inform targeted screening or prevention strategies.

3. Which factors show the strongest association with a previous breast cancer diagnosis, and how do they interact ?

Identifying the factors most strongly linked to prior breast cancer can help prioritize risk assessment and guide personalized patient management.

1.4 Related Work

Extensive research has established the role of demographic, reproductive, and clinical factors in shaping breast cancer risk. Classical epidemiological studies, such as those by Boyd et al. (2007) and McCormack & Santos Silva (2006), highlighted the strong association between breast density and breast cancer incidence, framing breast density as a key intermediate biomarker.

From a methodological standpoint, recent studies have leveraged data-driven and actuarial approaches to model cancer risk. Machine learning frameworks (e.g., Yala et al. (2022)) have demonstrated improved prediction of breast cancer outcomes using large-scale imaging and clinical datasets. These approaches align with actuarial principles of risk classification and model-based decision-making, emphasizing the integration of statistical learning into preventive health analytics.

The current study builds on this literature by focusing on exploratory and statistical modeling within an aggregated population dataset, emphasizing interpretability and visualization rather than prediction.

2. Data

2.1 Data Source

The dataset used in this study originates from the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (BCSC) (we accessed the dataset on october 2025).

The BCSC proposes three datasets corresponding to different collection periods:

- 2005-2009
- 2009-2013
- 2013-2017

These datasets were merged to obtain a unified dataset covering mammography data from 2005 to 2017. In total, it compiles information from 6,788,436 mammograms collected across multiple screening sites in the United States. Each record represents an aggregated population subgroup, defined by a unique combination of demographic, clinical, and reproductive characteristics. All data were fully anonymized prior to publication and are limited to U.S. populations. After filtering and quality checks, the resulting dataset includes 421,880 aggregated observations, each corresponding to a specific demographic and clinical profile.

2.4 Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

The preprocessing stage ensured that the dataset was clean, structured, and analytically reliable before conducting the exploratory and statistical analyses. Because the dataset originates from three distinct BCSC files merged into a single aggregated structure, precautions were required to guarantee consistency across demographic and clinical variables. All steps were performed in Python using pandas, ensuring full reproducibility.

1. Harmonization of categorical variables

To improve interpretability and ensure consistent category ordering during the analysis, the variables were recoded :

  • Using the BCSC documentation we changed the coded values into descriptive labels.

  • The categorical columns were converted to the category type to optimize memory efficiency and modeling compatibility.

  • Ordered variables (e.g. age, BMI, menopause) were converted into pandas ordered categorical types, which preserves clinical meaning and enables ordinal comparisons.

    e.g.: For BI-RADS, 1 → fatty; 2 → scattered; 3 → heterogeneously dense; 4 → extremely dense

This recoding step was crucial for both EDA and regression modelling, as many variables possess a natural clinical ordering that must be retained.

3. Construction of binary outcome variables

Two binary variables were created for the regression models:

  • BIRADS_high = 1 for dense breast tissue (BI-RADS categories 3–4), 0 otherwise.
  • breast_cancer_history_bin = 1 if the woman had a prior breast cancer diagnosis, 0 otherwise.

This transformation aligns the dataset with the research questions and ensures compatibility with logistic regression.

6. Re-encoding for statistical modelling

Because logistic regression requires numerical inputs, all categorical variables of the cleaned dataset needed to be re-encoded using:

  • One-hot encoding for nominal categories (family history, breast density)
  • Ordinal encoding for clinically ordered variables (age, BMI, menopause)

This step was directly informed by the EDA, which showed strong monotonic patterns (e.g., density decreases with age and BMI). Encoding therefore preserved clinically relevant structure and avoided inappropriate assumptions.

2.5 Challenges

While the dataset is comprehensive and large-scale, several challenges limit the scope of the analysis. The most significant limitation lies in its aggregated structure, which prevents patient-level investigation and reduces the granularity of possible analyses. Additionally, certain variables contain unknown or missing values, which may affect statistical robustness. Some combinations of demographic and clinical factors are rare, leading to potential sparsity in specific subgroups. Moreover, although the dataset includes information on race and ethnicity, this variable cannot be used for inferential analysis because of a strong overrepresentation of White non-Hispanic women, which would introduce substantial bias in population comparisons. These factors must be considered when interpreting the results of the exploratory analysis and logistic regression modeling.

We will explore more concretely these challenges in the EDA section.

3.4 Temporal trends

Figure 6 presents the temporal evolution of two key outcomes in the cohort between 2005 and 2017: the proportion of women with high breast density and the proportion with a prior breast cancer diagnosis.

The proportion of women with high breast density remains relatively stable over the twelve-year period, fluctuating within a narrow range around 0.48–0.52. The slight decrease observed after 2012 may reflect changes in the age distribution of screened women or shifts in reporting practices, but the overall stability indicates that breast density is a largely biologically driven trait, with no major cohort-wide changes over time.

In contrast, the proportion of women with a history of breast cancer shows a clear and gradual increase across the study period. This upward trend can be explained by multiple clinical and demographic factors: improved survivorship, earlier detection, increased longevity, and the accumulation of cancer cases as the cohort ages. Because prior breast cancer is an event that persists throughout life once diagnosed, its prevalence naturally rises over time in repeated screening cohorts.

Together, these patterns highlight two distinct dynamics: breast density behaves as a relatively stable biological characteristic, whereas the prevalence of prior breast cancer increases structurally over time. These temporal insights justify the inclusion of “year” only as a descriptive contextual variable and support focusing subsequent modelling on individual-level predictors rather than calendar time.

This focused correlation matrix (Figure 7) examines the relationships between key predictors after recoding categorical variables into a meaningful ordinal structure (e.g., pre → surgical → post menopause; lower → higher BMI). With recoded variables, the signs of correlations now carry interpretable directionality, allowing us to assess the strength and structure of associations relevant to breast density and breast cancer risk.

The strongest correlation observed is between age and menopausal status (r = 0.68), reflecting a biologically expected pattern: older women are far more likely to be post-menopausal. This strong positive relationship validates the recoding approach and confirms that menopausal status functions as a proxy for age-related hormonal changes.

A moderate positive correlation appears between age and prior breast cancer history (r = 0.21), consistent with established epidemiological evidence that breast cancer incidence increases with age. A smaller correlation is also observed between menopausal status and prior breast cancer (r = 0.16), further reinforcing this pathway.

Negative correlations involving breast density (e.g., age: r = –0.18, BMI: r = –0.28, menopause: r = –0.17) reflect well-documented physiological mechanisms: dense breast tissue is more common among younger, leaner, and pre-menopausal women. These values confirm the patterns identified in earlier bivariate plots and provide additional support for modeling high breast density separately from prior cancer history.

Finally, correlations involving family history remain close to zero, indicating that family history is not strongly associated with demographic or physiological variables. This independence suggests that family history may act as a unique genetic risk factor, justifying its inclusion as a distinct predictor in subsequent statistical models.

Overall, the recoded correlation matrix confirms the absence of problematic multicollinearity among the selected predictors, supports the biological plausibility of the observed relationships, and validates the methodological choice to include age, BMI, menopausal status, family history, and breast density as separate explanatory variables in the next stage of analysis.

3.6 Key Findings from EDA

The exploratory analysis provides a structured and clinically consistent overview of the factors associated with high breast density and prior breast cancer, the two central outcomes of this study. Each exhibit was selected to highlight a specific determinant, and together they reveal coherent physiological, demographic, and temporal patterns that strongly guide the subsequent modelling strategy.

First, Exhibits 1 and 2 clearly delineate the risk profile associated with high breast density. Exhibit 1 shows a strong inverse gradient between BMI and breast density, with leaner women exhibiting substantially higher proportions of BI-RADS 3–4 categories. Exhibit 2 complements this by demonstrating a sharp and monotonic decline of breast density with age. These two visualizations jointly confirm that dense breast tissue is concentrated among younger, low-BMI, predominantly pre-menopausal women. This pattern is biologically well-established: adipose tissue increasingly replaces glandular tissue with age and weight gain. The consistency of these gradients across the cleaned dataset reinforces breast density as a biomarker strongly shaped by hormonal status and intrinsic breast composition, validating age and BMI as essential predictors in all subsequent models.

Second, Exhibit 3 focuses on prior breast cancer and highlights the pronounced differences across menopausal categories. Post-menopausal women exhibit the highest prevalence of past breast cancer, followed by women with surgical menopause. This distinction is clinically meaningful: surgical menopause often results from conditions requiring oophorectomy or hysterectomy, which abruptly modify hormonal exposure, whereas natural menopause reflects gradual endogenous decline. Pre-menopausal women show the lowest prevalence, consistent with shorter cumulative exposure to estrogen and a younger age structure. This exhibit therefore provides a clear justification for treating natural and surgical menopause as distinct categories and for integrating menopausal status into the predictive modelling of breast cancer risk.

Third, Exhibit 4 underscores the magnitude of family history as a prominent, non-modifiable risk factor. Women with a first-degree family history of breast cancer show nearly three times the prevalence of prior cancer compared with women without such history. This is one of the strongest associations observed in the EDA and aligns with hereditary susceptibility pathways, including BRCA-related mechanisms and shared environmental or behavioral exposures. The strength and clarity of this pattern justify the central role of family history in all multivariate analyses.

Fourth, Exhibit 5 provides temporal insight from 2005 to 2017 and reveals two distinct dynamics. The proportion of women with high breast density remains relatively stable over time, confirming its nature as a biologically determined trait rather than a cohort-driven phenomenon. In contrast, the prevalence of prior breast cancer increases gradually across the years, reflecting improvements in detection, survivorship, and the accumulation of cancer cases in an aging screening population. After data cleaning, these temporal trends are smooth and coherent, with no anomalies or discontinuities, reinforcing the reliability of the longitudinal structure of the dataset.

Finally, the EDA identifies structural characteristics of the dataset that influence the interpretation of results. The population shows a marked overrepresentation of non-Hispanic white women, which limits the generalizability of ethnicity-related comparisons and requires cautious interpretation of racial differences. Additionally, the focused correlation analysis on recoded variables indicates low multicollinearity, confirming the appropriateness of multivariate logistic regression and the stability of coefficient estimates.

Overall, the EDA provides a comprehensive and biologically grounded picture. High breast density is primarily associated with younger age, lower BMI, and pre-menopausal status, while prior breast cancer is linked to older age, menopausal transition (natural or surgical), and family history. These insights justify modelling the two outcomes separately and confirm the relevance of including age, BMI, menopausal status, and family history as core predictors in the upcoming statistical analysis. The coherence of all exhibits, combined with the absence of data irregularities after cleaning, ensures a robust foundation for the development of accurate predictive models and meaningful risk stratification.

4. Analysis

4.1 Statistical Methods selected

We employ multivariate logistic regression to analyze the determinants of two binary outcomes: high breast density (BI-RADS 3–4) and prior breast cancer history. Logistic regression accommodates categorical and ordinal predictors and provides interpretable effect estimates (odds ratios). Two preliminary models are therefore constructed: one predicting high breast density and one predicting prior breast cancer.

4.2. Rationale for the Method Choice

The selection of logistic regression is guided directly by findings from the exploratory analysis. The EDA revealed strong univariate patterns—such as the inverse association between age and breast density and the elevated prevalence of prior breast cancer in post-menopausal women, which require multivariable adjustment to isolate independent effects. Predictor variables (age, BMI, menopausal status, breast density, and family history) are categorical or ordinal, and correlation analysis confirmed the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Logistic regression is therefore an appropriate and stable method for modelling these relationships.

4.3 Analytical Objectives

The models aim to quantify the contribution of demographic, hormonal, and anthropometric factors to breast density and prior breast cancer history. They evaluate whether the visual patterns observed in the EDA persist after adjustment, identify potential interaction effects, and provide interpretable effect sizes to support the construction of risk profiles. In this way, the analysis transforms descriptive trends into statistically supported insights relevant for risk stratification and clinical interpretation.

The regression model (Table 9) quantifies the independent effects of demographic, hormonal, and reproductive factors on the likelihood of presenting high breast density (BI-RADS 3–4). The results reveal several strong and clinically consistent patterns.

1. Age is the strongest predictor of breast density

All age groups show large negative coefficients, indicating substantially lower odds of high breast density compared with women aged 18–29 (reference group). The effect becomes progressively stronger with age:

  • Women aged 30–34 already show significantly lower odds.
  • Women aged 55–59, for example, have a coefficient of –1.19, indicating much lower likelihood.
  • Women aged 85+ have the lowest odds (coef ≈ –1.96).

This monotonic decrease reflects the physiological decline in glandular tissue and the gradual replacement by adipose tissue over the life course.

2. BMI is inversely associated with breast density

Both overweight and obese categories display significant negative coefficients:

  • BMI 25–29.99: –0.47
  • BMI 30–34.99: –0.87
  • BMI 35+: –1.34 (strongest effect)

This confirms that higher BMI is associated with lower breast density because adiposity increases the proportion of fatty tissue in the breast.

3. Menopausal status has a strong hormonal effect

Using post-menopausal women as the reference: - Pre-menopausal women have significantly higher odds of dense breasts (coef = +0.178). - Surgical menopause shows a small positive effect (coef = +0.098), likely reflecting earlier abrupt hormonal changes.

This aligns with the biological role of estrogen: pre-menopausal women tend to have denser tissue due to higher circulating hormones.

4. Reproductive history also contributes

Compared with the reference category (age at first birth 20–24):

  • First birth at <20 is associated with lower odds of dense breasts (coef = –0.098).
  • First birth at 25–29 and ≥30 are associated with slightly higher odds (coef ≈ +0.05 to +0.06).
  • Nulliparous women (never gave birth) have significantly higher odds (coef = +0.168).

These effects are weaker than age or BMI but consistent with known reproductive influences on breast composition.
